I’d prioritize four general improvements:

1. Deduplicate evidence across the whole negotiation. Treat closely related forecasts as one argument unless a later one adds genuinely new information.

We should address each failure with a general rule based on evidence and economics, not a special case for P07, P11, or any other supplier.

1. Repeated arguments: Track evidence already used during the whole negotiation, not just within one email. Group semantically related facts, such as overlapping forecasts for the same part and period, and use the strongest one once. Allow a later fact only if it adds new information. That fixes P07’s repetition without discarding useful new evidence.
2. Repeated or ineffective offers: Don’t send the same price and terms again. Detect when our offer hasn’t meaningfully changed or the supplier’s counter has stopped moving. Then evaluate a useful alternative package or decide whether the best standing offer is acceptable based on total cost and must-haves. A “closing” label alone should not trigger a higher offer; it should be supported by actual movement or a credible final position. That addresses P11 without hard-coding its price or a P11-specific rule.
3. Missed supplier needs: Turn explicit needs into conditional, costed trades. If a supplier values earlier payment, calculate whether offering it earns enough price improvement to justify our cost. If it needs volume certainty, evaluate a firm commitment against the supply benefit and contract risk. Compare complete packages, not just unit prices, and keep every option inside our cost and must-have limits.
4. Writer and research timeouts: Treat model-written text and analysis as optional. Give each task a firm deadline, validate its output, and fall back to a concise template when it fails or arrives late. Track timeout, rejection, and fallback rates across sessions so we fix recurring failure types rather than tuning prompts to one email.

To avoid overfitting, validate changes in stages: replay the full local case set across varied runs, then compare baseline and candidate on the same hosted cases with run order alternated and multiple samples. Review per-case changes and regressions alongside the average. Keep a change only when the gain repeats across cases or supplier patterns; don’t promote it because one score jumped.
