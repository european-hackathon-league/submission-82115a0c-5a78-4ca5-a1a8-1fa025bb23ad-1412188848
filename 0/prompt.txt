Bargaining power comes from patience, so estimate each supplier’s cost of delay.
With both sides equally patient (δ = 0.9), the proposer gets only 53 % of the surplus. Against an impatient supplier (δ = 0.7), it gets 81 %.
Our data shows impatience:
cash-short signals: Wälzhammer’s and Weißacher’s credit indices, short-time work
free capacity advertised: Fuchsreiter, Ilzauer
how much of its turnover we are
its own reply delay and quote validity
A patient supplier (full order book, near-monopolist) won’t move much on price, so go for non-price levers.
Turn that into the capture target. Instead of a guessed 0.65–0.8, compute a share from the estimated patience of both sides and clamp it by tier and goal. This directly addresses your open “capture is still a guess” problem, and it’s explainable to the jury.
Our own patience falls during a wave. Fewer rounds left and a closer cap mean a lower δ for us, so concede faster or accept. A strong alternative raises our patience. The closing rule follows from the model: continue only while the expected gain from one more round exceeds its cost (walk risk times what we’d lose, plus time).
Delay is pure loss. In equilibrium, agreement is immediate, and in the arena every round costs about 60–75 s. Against an impatient supplier, make the first counter close to where you expect to land rather than an extreme anchor followed by many small steps. Every round must carry information, such as the probe sheets.
Proposer advantage. The supplier’s opening quote is its proposal, but our first counter is the reference it reacts to. Make that counter complete, with all fields at our preferred values. Anything left open closes on the supplier’s standard terms.
Only credible threats count. Subgame perfection rules out threats you wouldn’t carry out. Imply a walk-away only when our alternative actually beats the standing offer. The bots read signals the way the model assumes rational players do, so bluffs cost us.
Where the model stops fitting is where the arena game actually is. The paper notes that when costs are private, delays appear and players pretend to be stronger. That is the arena: “that’s not our reality” and “final offer” can be posturing. So estimate a supplier’s patience from data, not from what it says. This is the same logic as the fact checker.
