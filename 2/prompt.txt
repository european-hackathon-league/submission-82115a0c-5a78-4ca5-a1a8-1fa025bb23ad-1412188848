  1. First-offer advantage. Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001), JPSP 81(4), 657-669, doi 10.1037/0022-3514.81.4.657. Across 3 experiments the party making the first offer (buyer or seller) got the better outcome, and first offers strongly predicted settlement. The advantage disappeared when the other side focused on information cutting against the offer (opponent's alternatives, opponent's reservation price, own target). Found in face-to-face and email. Source: https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/First-offers-as-anchors:-the-role-of-and-negotiator-Galinsky-Mussweiler/4593e56e2f94f5ffc75789ddacdfbe3670481cbe. Effect sizes not verified.
  
  2. Precise vs round anchors. Mason, Lee, Wiley & Ames (2013), JESP 49(4), 759-763. Precise offers (e.g. $5,015 vs $5,000) made the offerer look better informed, and recipients conceded more. Source: https://business.columbia.edu/press-release/cbs-press-releases/new-research-shows-asking-precise-not-round-number-during. A Psychology Today figure of counteroffers 24% closer to the precise opener is secondary and unverified. Loschelder et al. (2017, JESP 70, 164-176) found precision raises anchor potency for recipients but senders choose less extreme precise offers. PON summary says 49 studies found more precision means stronger anchoring, mostly with amateurs; experts are less moved: https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/dealmaking-daily/negotiation-research-can-use-effective-first-offer-strive-precision-nb. No effect size found.

Walk-aways: 30.3% (10/33) after an extreme offer vs 11.4% after a moderate one. Low-power negotiators walked; high-power were equally offended but stayed. Source: https://www.martinschweinsberg.com/content/files/2022/03/Publication-2012-Schweinsberg-Ku-Wang-Pillutla-JESP-in-press.pdf. The term "insult zone" did not appear in results; the replication (JDM 2018) also found more impasses after extreme openers: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167487018302642.

Recipients of shrinking concessions made less ambitious counters and, in one study, worse deals. Effect strongest at moderate shrink rates over more rounds; a recipient target price protected them. Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v165y2021icp153-166.html.

  1. Open 15-20% below quote, not 30%+. Schweinsberg's walk-away jump and our practice result point the same way. Test 15/20/25 in the sim first.
  2. Use a precise, non-round opener (e.g. 17.4%, not 20%), attached to a cost reason. Precision evidence is lab-based with amateurs; treat as a hypothesis.
  3. Make the opening move within the first 1-2 rounds; the first-offer advantage holds unless the supplier knows our alternative, so keep alternatives hidden.
  4. Do not shrink concessions toward zero by round 3-4. Keep steps roughly equal or slightly growing early, then a small final step. Direction is contested (finding 4 vs 5); check the sim.
  5. Cap concessions at about 3 moves before the supplier's price floor is likely hit; this is a guess, not sourced.
  6. Attach a reason to each concession (cost driver, volume, payment days). Reasons cost nothing and fit the precision/justification intuition.
  7. Do not accept at the first acceptable number if rounds remain; one counter costs nothing and the best achievable price sat 7-22% lower.
  8. Send an explicit final offer only once, near round 8-9, and keep it at a price the agent can still accept. Its credibility is unsourced.
  9. Track walk-away risk after any offer that is more than about 25% below quote; the replication data suggest impasse risk rises past that point.
  10. Log the supplier's reply length and tone per round to test the offense hypothesis in our own sessions.

  **Proposed rules for a 10-round buyer (design guidance, not from a source unless stated)**
  1. Concession curve: u(r) = R + (Open - R) * (1 - r/10)^k with k around 2-3, so we hold early and move in rounds 6-10. Reservation R = alternative cost (or walk-away value).

  Research on multi-supplier negotiation (web only, no files edited). Flagged uncertainties are marked.
  
  **Key findings**
  1. Bulow & Klemperer, "Auctions Versus Negotiations," AER 86(1) 1996, pp. 180-194. Under standard assumptions, an auction with no reserve beats an optimally structured negotiation if it attracts at least one extra bidder, even though it gives up the seller's negotiating power. Extra competition is worth more than negotiating skill. Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w4608 (abstract checked). Uncertain: the abstract-level sources do not confirm the affiliated-signals caveat. Its result is for a single-object seller with bidders, not a buyer with 2-3 suppliers, so the transfer is a heuristic.
  2. Bajari, McMillan & Tadelis, JLEO 25(2) 2009. For customized, complex contracts with incomplete design and few bidders, negotiation beats auctions. Auctions block design-stage information exchange. Bilateral negotiation wins when design improvements matter, renegotiation is costly, and the buyer's position is strong. Source: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=414255 (abstract only). This matches our setting: specs vary per supplier (overtime, capacity, index clause), so a pure price auction is the wrong tool.
  3. Multi-party concurrent negotiation (Nguyen & Jennings 2004-2005; Rahwan, Kowalczyk & Pham 2002). A coordinator runs one thread per supplier. Their work covers coordination heuristics and decommitment with a penalty when a settled deal is dropped. Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10726-014-9379-5 ; https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/1159.pdf (2025 survey, taxonomy of interdependency between threads). Uncertain: I did not find a multi-attribute Thomas/Nguyen/Jennings paper; the search did not surface it.
  4. BAFO. Vendor sources only: one closing round where all shortlisted suppliers submit a final price and terms at once. One anecdote reports about 3% improvement. Repeated BAFOs teach suppliers to withhold their best number. BAFO is weak for very few viable suppliers. Source: https://www.tacto.ai/en/procurement-glossary/best-and-final-offer-bafo ; https://www.zycus.com/glossary/what-is-best-and-final-offer. No peer-reviewed evidence found.
  5. Reverse auctions. Emiliani & Stec (2002) argue headline savings are overstated; about half of GE's savings evaporated through data errors, post-auction haggling and spec changes; suppliers forced into auctions retaliate later. Beall et al. (2003) cite about 15% average savings. Both are contested and not peer-reviewed in our search. Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/REDACTED
  6. Shopping a quote ("can you beat X?"). Practitioner guidance treats it as standard, and a competing quote on the same spec sets the reference price. Public-sector guidance says an unusually low competitor bid can signal risk. No outcome study found. Source: https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/procurement2021/chapter/chapter-5-evaluating-price/
  7. Not found: BATNA and losing-bidder research for multiple suppliers, and sequencing studies. I have no sourced evidence for those topics.

  1. Keep every thread's numbers in code. Compare offers on total_cost, not unit price, so overtime, capacity and lead time are priced in before any comparison.
  2. Cite a rival only by real standing offer ID. Use it as a reference price, not a bluff. Do not claim a rival's number you cannot cite.
  3. Push the most price-sensitive supplier first, and keep the others warm with a non-price question (capacity, lead time, index clause). Sequencing evidence is absent, so treat this as a hypothesis.
  4. Keep losing threads open until a deal is signed. Walking away from a thread early removes your BATNA. Message budget (10 per thread) is a real constraint, so reserve 1-2 messages for a final check.

  Research report: opponent modelling and reservation-price estimation
  
  Source access: I could not read full texts. The Zeng & Sycara and IJCAI PDFs returned no extractable text, and Springer redirects to a login. Everything sourced below comes from search snippets and secondary descriptions. Section 3 is my own derivation, not a source.
  
  1. Sources and what they say
  - Zeng & Sycara, "Bayesian Learning in Negotiation" (IJHCS 1998; 1996 workshop version: https://cdn.aaai.org/Symposia/Spring/1996/SS-96-01/SS96-01-019.pdf). Buyer keeps a set of hypotheses on the seller's reserve price, weights them by likelihood given the offer history, and uses the posterior-weighted estimate. Secondary sources say it covers only single-issue cases. Oldest and most cited reservation-value learner.
  - Baarslag, Hendrikx, Hindriks, Jonker, "Learning about the opponent in automated bilateral negotiation" (AAMAS 2016; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10458-015-9309-1). Taxonomy of opponent models by learning technique. Covers acceptance-strategy learning and bidding-strategy forecasting as a time series. Use it as the entry point; I did not read the sections on curve fitting or deception.
  - Hou, "Predicting agents tactics in automated negotiation" (IAT 2004, pp. 127-133). Assumes the opponent uses a time-, behaviour- or resource-dependent tactic and fits its parameters by non-linear regression on past offers. A Hou tech report (https://kmi.open.ac.uk/publications/papers/kmi-tr-144.pdf) says this predicts the opponent's deadline and reservation value. Later papers call it valid only for pure tactics from those families.
  - Williams, Robu, Gerding, Jennings, IAMhaggler (IJCAI 2011, https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/11/Papers/080.pdf; 2013 chapter). Gaussian process regression predicts the opponent's future concessions, with uncertainty; the agent then optimises its own concession against that prediction. Kernel and exact formulas unverified.
  - Frequency models (Bayesian/frequency acceptance learning) are covered in the Baarslag survey; not checked in detail.
  - "Concession bait" detection: I found no source. Treat everything in section 4 as untested.
  
  2. Hard constraint (solid from the problem setup, not a citation)
  Seller offers only fall toward its floor R_s. So R_s <= p_n for every observed offer p_n. This is a hard upper bound on the floor. Use it as a clip on every estimate.
  
  3. Algorithm sketch (my derivation)
  Let p_1..p_n be the supplier's price offers in order (the supplier's price falls toward its floor R).
  - Drops: d_i = p_{i-1} - p_i. Ratio: r_i = d_{i+1}/d_i.
  - Geometric model: p_i = R + A r^i. Three points fix A, r, R exactly (Aitken delta-squared):
    R = p_3 - (p_3 - p_2)^2 / ((p_3 - p_2) - (p_2 - p_1))
  - Remaining-drops view: if drops shrink geometrically with ratio r and m supplier moves remain, the total further concession is d_n * (r + r^2 + ... + r^m). The infinite sum is d_n * r / (1 - r) when r < 1.
  - Clip: R_hat = min(R_hat, p_n) and R_hat >= 0 (or a cost-based floor from arena_kit).
  
  Worked check on the example 52.90, 51.90, 51.40, 51.10, 50.90:
  - Drops: 1.00, 0.50, 0.30, 0.20. Ratios: 0.50, 0.60, 0.67.
  - Aitken on (52.90, 51.90, 51.40) gives 50.90.
  - Aitken on (51.40, 51.10, 50.90) gives 50.50.
  - Drops look like 1/i (1, 0.5, 0.33, 0.25). That harmonic shape has a divergent sum, so no finite floor follows from extrapolation.
  
  Consequence: the ratio is rising, so the true decay is slower than geometric. Aitken then underestimates the floor and pushes too hard. Use the conservative (higher) estimate for the stop rule, and prefer a fitted power law p_i = R + A i^(-b) (non-linear least squares, 3-4 points, b > 0) as a second opinion.
  
  4. Concession bait vs real floor (untested heuristics)
  - Bait: a large first step that later drops sharply, or steps that do not respond to our move size.
  - Real floor signal: steps shrink and then stop after our counter-move that the supplier's notes say it cannot meet.
  - Reciprocity test: compare the supplier's step to our last step. A reactive supplier concedes in proportion; a fixed script does not.
  - Stop rule: walk when the conservative R_hat is within our cost margin of the alternative.
  
  5. Failure modes
  - 2-4 points cannot separate geometric, power-law and bait; the estimate is high-variance.
  - The supplier is an LLM persona, not a formula; it may concede for reasons unrelated to price (deadline, relationship).
  - Non-monotone offers (reversals, re-quotes) break the drop model.
  - Hou's method assumes the family is right; a mismatch gives a confident wrong floor.
  - Zeng & Sycara's hypothesis grid needs a prior; the result depends on it.
  
  6. Uncertain
  Whether the 2004 Hou paper and the tech report are the same work; IAMhaggler's exact kernel; whether the Baarslag survey's acceptance-strategy section applies to a 10-message cap. Verify against the full PDFs before relying on any formula beyond sections 2 and 3.

  **Bargaining research: key findings**
  
  Several sources were only available as search snippets, not full texts, so treat the details below as provisional.
  
  1. **Rubinstein (1982), alternating offers.** Unique subgame-perfect outcome, with the first mover's share x = (1−δ₂)/(1−δ₁δ₂). With a common discount factor δ this gives 1/(1+δ) for the proposer, which tends to 1/2 as δ→1. The symmetric simplification is my algebra, not a quoted result. Sources: https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/67859/1/MPRA_paper_67859.pdf, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05876
  2. **Deadline effect.** Roth, Murnighan & Schoumaker (1988, AER 78(4)) report agreements bunching just before the deadline across four experiments. Later models note the effect depends on the deadline being at least partly certain. Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/bargaining-under-time-pressure-from-deadlines/F388EC46629F094DDB48A96AE00F8DCC
  3. **Fershtman & Seidmann (1993), JET 60, 306–321.** Delay until the deadline is inefficient when a rejecting party is committed to refuse poorer later offers. Small cost of delay plus an "ultimatum" framing restores the seller's power. Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10683-018-9579-y (secondary); DOI 10.1006/jeth.1993.1045. I did not verify the model's exact assumptions.
  4. **Ultimatum rejection.** Oosterbeek, Sloof & van de Kuilen meta-analysis: average offers ≈40% of the pie, average rejection ≈16%. Narrative reviews put offers near or below 20% at roughly 50% rejection, and about 80% at 12.5%. The "below 20%" figures come from reviews, not the meta-analysis. Sources: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/REDACTED, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331443680_Stake_size_effects_in_ultimatum_game_and_dictator_game_offers_A_meta-analysis
  5. **Coase conjecture.** Gul, Sonnenschein & Wilson (1986, JET 39): with patient buyers, a durable-goods monopolist cannot hold price above competitive levels. Outside options can break it (Board & Pycia 2014, AER). Source: https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Foundations-of-Dynamic-Monopoly-and-the-Coase-Gul-Sonnenschein/02b7e3c623b846f54bd56f82c59bfca4f890b7b4
  
  **Not covered:** war-of-attrition concession dynamics, Schelling commitment, and screening under one-sided incomplete information. I did not search these, so no sourced claims are made.
  
  **Implications (heuristics, not tested in our data):**
  
  1. **Do not reveal the clock.** Deadline clustering means the supplier's concessions bunch where it expects our deadline. Keep the 8-minute and 10-message limits out of every e-mail.
  2. **Open near target, not low.** Ultimatum evidence suggests offers below roughly 20% of the surplus risk rejection at about 50%. Anchor the first sheet at a fair-split point, not a lowball, so breakdown risk stays low early.
  3. **Front-load moderate, then shrink.** Use decreasing increments, for example gap multipliers of about 0.6 per round, so each step looks like the last real concession. Reserve the largest step for the penultimate message, not the last, if the supplier is near its floor.
  4. **Accept rule.** Accept an offer now if its cost C_offer ≤ (1−p)·E[C_next] + p·C_alt, where p is our estimated breakdown probability for another round and C_alt is the alternative. With p ≈ 0.1–0.2 and continuation gains small, accept sooner.
  5. **Final-message sheet must be acceptable to us.** Near the deadline, the supplier's best response is often a take-it-or-leave-it offer. Compute the sheet that is worst-acceptable with `total_cost` and `must_haves_ok` before message 9, so a final_offer can be taken immediately.
  6. **Use the 3-sheet menu to screen.** Offering two or three sheets with different term mixes (price vs payment days vs volume) reveals the supplier's preferences without a concession. This is my inference from screening logic, not from the sources above.
  7. **Commitment only if credible.** Stating "final" moves the outcome only if we actually walk. Rival price claims (verifiable, ID-backed) work better as a substitute for an unverifiable threat.
  8. **Keep the fallback private unless verified.** Outside options shift outcomes (Board & Pycia), but an unsupported BATNA claim is a `contradicted` risk under the claim-check rules.
  
  **Uncertain:** the 0.6 multiplier and the p ≈ 0.1–0.2 breakdown estimate are placeholders to calibrate against logged sessions, not findings.

Payment reliability is supported only by vendor surveys (Taulia, Primerevenue). Treat as weak.
  
  3. Collaborative vs competitive negotiation. Vantage Partners (271 buy-side and 259 sell-side respondents, self-reported, correlational): collaborative negotiators report better relations and fewer implementation problems. Over 60% of the top 10% of value realisers described their negotiations as collaborative. High performers were assertive and collaborative at once. Suppliers give more value to customers who negotiate collaboratively during implementation. Source: https://www.vantagepartners.com/insights/customer-supplier-negotiation-study. Flag: "28% higher deal value" and "15-25%" figures circulate online and I could not trace either to a primary source. Do not cite them.
  
  4. Supply chain finance / reverse factoring. A bank pays the supplier early on invoices the buyer has approved. Pricing rests on the buyer's credit, so the supplier borrows more cheaply. The buyer pays the full amount on the original due date, and the buyer's balance sheet is unaffected (unlike dynamic discounting). Sources are vendor glossaries, so figures are illustrative: https://taulia.com/de/glossary/what-is-reverse-factoring/ and https://paystreamadvisors.com/blog/reverse-factoring/.
  
  5. Prepayment is a last resort. University of Toronto policy advises avoiding prepayment where possible because quality or delivery problems are hard to remedy once paid: https://procurement.utoronto.ca/?p=11788. Insurance exists (e.g., Euler Hermes Advance Payment Protection): https://www.allianz.com/en/press/news/business/insurance/REDACTED/. Zhao and Huchzermeier (2019) compare advance-payment discounts with buyer-backed PO financing for a distressed SME supplier. Its preferred option depends on the buyer's asset level. I did not open the paper.
  
  6. Distress monitoring. Financial health is often checked only at onboarding, while distress can develop within a few quarters: https://creditriskmonitor.com/resources/blog-posts/how-procurement-professionals-successfully-manage-supplier-financial-risk. UK government guidance: assess standing to decide which safeguards close the gap: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/987132/Assessing_and_monitoring_the_economic_and_financial_standing_of_suppliers_guidance_note_May_2021.pdf. Penalty clauses can worsen supplier finances, so limit them.

This is the closest academic analogue to "customer of choice." Source: https://research.iimb.ac.in/fac_pubs/620. Kraljic and bottleneck items: secure supply first via delivery or payment contracts, buffers and capacity guarantees, then reduce dependence. Bidding is the wrong tool. Source: https://business.amazon.com/en/blog/kraljic-matrix. Flag: some sources swap the Kraljic axes; I used the standard framing.
  
  8. Open-book / cost-plus. Open-book lets the buyer verify a fair price, mainly in cost-plus or thin-competition settings. It works only with exercised audit rights and tight cost definitions, or suppliers can inflate the base: https://www.publicspendforum.net/blogs/peter-smith/2017/08/21/open-book-contracts-supplier-public-procurement and https://www.tacto.ai/en/procurement-glossary-downloads/open-book-costing.
  
  9. Limited authority / approval ladder. No controlled study found. Practitioner sources (Harvard PON, Karrass) advise clarifying counterpart authority early, and warn that "I must check with my boss" often comes back with extra asks. Source: https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/business-negotiations/how-much-authority-do-they-have/?amp. Treat as heuristic only.
  
  RULES (proposed; the evidence above supports 1-3 well, 4-10 less so)
  
  1. Offer forecast visibility first: a rolling 12-week forecast with a stated accuracy band. Cheap for us, the most-cited supplier want.
  2. Offer a volume commitment within a band (for example, a minimum share over the horizon). It is the currency most tied to capacity allocation (finding 7). Do not offer it unless the coverage score supports it.
  3. Offer payment reliability: a fixed payment date, not a faster one. Pay on time and show it. Use reverse factoring rather than our own early payment if the supplier is cash-short (finding 4).
  4. Prefer supply-chain finance over prepayment. Offer prepayment only with insurance or a delivery guarantee, and only when the score gains exceed the cash risk (finding 5).
  5. Trade "low hassle" for price: fewer change orders, a single order channel, standard terms. Keep it as an assumption; it is not sourced.
  6. Ask for capacity reservation or priority allocation in exchange for a longer contract or a volume floor. This is the bottleneck logic (finding 7).
  7. Compute the supplier's sustainable margin from its own cost structure. Offer open-book access on cost lines with audit rights. Do not accept an open-book claim without a verifiable cost base (finding 8).
  8. Price the supplier's margin gap into the score: if a sheet leaves the supplier below its sustainable margin, the charge rises with the square of the gap. Use non-price currencies to close that gap instead of price.
  9. Before a large move, ask whether the supplier's approver must sign off, and what they need to take back. Give the representative a defensible story to carry upward (finding 9). Do not reveal our walk-away.
  10. Keep concessions collaborative in tone while holding firm on numbers. Vantage supports this combination (finding 3), but the evidence is correlational.

  2. **Practical MESO guidance (Harvard PON, Medvec and Galinsky).** Present about three equivalent offers, "avoid presenting more than three." Build a weighted scoring system so offers of different makeup have equal value to you. Keep every offer above your target. Reactions to offers reveal priorities and their strength. A savvy counterpart may cherry-pick across offers; answer with three new equivalent offers. Source: https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/business-negotiations/how-to-use-mesos-in-business-negotiations
  3. **Similarity-based trade-offs (Faratin, Sierra, Jennings 2002).** "Using similarity criteria to make issue trade-offs in automated negotiations," Artificial Intelligence 142, pp. 205-237. The agent is indifferent along an iso-utility curve, so it proposes the point on that curve most similar to the opponent's last offer. It uses fuzzy similarity and hill-climbing. Source (search snippet only): https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/fc2ffe91-2e01-463e-a64b-8b633e74c5ba
  4. **Lai, Sycara, Li (2008).** Multiagent and Grid Systems 4(1):45-65. Up to k offers per round, all on the current iso-utility curve, chosen by Euclidean-distance similarity to the opponent's last offer. Reported to get close to the Pareto frontier. Only a secondary description was found; the Lai and Sycara paper itself was not located.
  5. **Opponent preference learning (Hindriks and Tykhonov, AAMAS 2008).** Bayesian posterior over preference and issue-weight hypotheses, updated per bid, assuming a concession-based opponent. Source: https://tracinsy.ewi.tudelft.nl/pubtrac/GeniusWeb/wiki/OpponentModels. Alternative: Coehoorn and Jennings, kernel density over concession differences, with no history assumption (same source).
  6. **Post-settlement settlements.** The search attributes the term to Raiffa, Negotiation Journal 1985 (vol. 1, pp. 9-12). Critique in Journal of Dispute Resolution 1996: https://scholarship.law.missouri.edu/jdr/vol1996/iss1/6/
  7. **Nothing agreed until everything agreed.** Attributed to Fisher and Ury, Getting to Yes, in a secondary source. Package bargaining links issues and can move the process from distributive to integrative. Sources: https://www.goodreads.com/notes/11820482-getting-to-yes/12925436-annie/846fc426-0d78-4347-bb7f-c7d9951de628 and https://storyboardthat.com/ro/articles/b/negociere-cu-pachete
  
  **Rules for the three sheets per message**
  
  1. Send exactly three sheets, each equal in our total_cost (within a small tolerance). Equal to us, different in mix.
  2. Vary only the issues where the supplier's value diverges from ours (for example payment days against unit price, start date against lead time). Do not vary issues where we and they agree.
  3. Put all three above our target and walk-away. The first set should be more aggressive than the target, and the sheets should not reveal that they are equal.
  4. Score every sheet with total_cost and must_haves_ok, and fill every negotiable field (standard terms for unstated fields), so equivalence holds on the full sheet.
  5. After the supplier picks, update the belief over its issue weights (Bayesian or simple counts). Treat the choice as a revealed priority, and its counters as the strength of that priority.
  6. Next message: generate three new equal-score sheets on the iso-utility curve nearest the supplier's last offer (similarity by normalised Euclidean distance over issues). Do not repeat the previous set.
  7. If the supplier combines parts of several sheets, treat that as a signal of cherry-picking. Re-run the scoring and answer with three new equivalent sheets rather than accepting the mix unless total_cost passes our bar.
  8. Stop offering new sheets once a sheet hits the accept threshold; accept the best of the chosen set rather than widening it.
